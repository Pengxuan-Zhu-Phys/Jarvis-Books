\chapter{The Likelihood}
\label{ch:loglikelihood}

\newthought{The likelihood closes the per-point loop.} After the workflow has filled the
observables dictionary, a list of named expressions turns it into a score. This chapter
covers where those expressions live, how the total \code{LogL} is formed, and what happens
when evaluation fails.

\section{Where likelihood terms live}
\label{sec:likelihood-blocks}

One home: \yamlkey{Sampling.LogLikelihood} (alias \yamlkey{loglikelihood}), a list of
named expressions.

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  LogLikelihood:
    - name: LogL_Z
      expression: LogGauss(z, 1.0, 0.1)
    - name: LogL_mass
      expression: -0.5 * ((mh - 125.25) / 0.17)**2
\end{yamlwin}

It sits inside \yamlkey{Sampling} for a reason: the score is what a sampler steers on, so
the likelihood belongs to the same block that chooses how the space gets explored ---
Section~\ref{sec:sampling-loglikelihood} places it among the other decisions that block
asks you for. A \yamlkey{Likelihood} block at the top level of the card is not a \Jtwo\
interface and is rejected on load, with a message pointing here.

Each entry:

\begin{keylist}
  \item[name] \opt, default \code{LogL}. The term's observable name; results are added
        to the observables dictionary and appear in the output records.
  \item[expression] \req. An expression in the shared language of
        Chapter~\ref{ch:expressions}, evaluated over the observables.
\end{keylist}

\section{Writing the physics down}
\label{sec:likelihood-physics}

The mechanics above are easy; deciding \emph{what to write} is the actual work. Almost
every constraint you want to impose falls into one of four shapes, and it is worth being
able to recognise which one you are holding before reaching for a formula.

\subsection*{A measurement with a symmetric error}

The common case: an experiment reports $\hat{x} \pm \sigma$, and your model predicts
\code{x}. The Gaussian log-kernel is the whole term.

\begin{yamlwin}
- {name: LogL_mh, expression: "LogGauss(mh, 125.25, 0.17)"}
\end{yamlwin}

Use the \emph{total} uncertainty here --- experimental and theoretical added in
quadrature. A prediction from a one-loop calculation carries a theory error that is
frequently larger than the measurement's, and omitting it makes your allowed region
narrower than your physics can justify. If the theory error is itself a parameter you
would rather fit than guess, that is what \yamlkey{Sampling.Nuisance} is for
(Section~\ref{sec:nuisance}).

\subsection*{An asymmetric error}

When the reported interval is $\hat{x}\,^{+\sigma_+}_{-\sigma_-}$, pick the side by the
sign of the residual. The shared language has no ternary, but \code{Heaviside} does the
job, and writing the two halves as named terms keeps it readable:

\begin{yamlwin}
- {name: d_bsg,  expression: "BR_bsg - 3.32e-4"}
- {name: LogL_bsg,
   expression: "-0.5 * d_bsg**2 / (Heaviside(d_bsg) * 0.25e-4**2
                + Heaviside(-d_bsg) * 0.33e-4**2)"}
\end{yamlwin}

\subsection*{A one-sided exclusion limit}

An upper limit is not a measurement, and forcing it into a Gaussian is a common way to
get a wrong answer. What you usually want is a term that is flat where the model is
allowed and falls off where it is excluded. If the limit is a single number, a step is
enough:

\begin{yamlwin}
- {name: LogL_limit,
   expression: "-1e6 * Heaviside(sigma_SI - 1.2e-46)"}
\end{yamlwin}

But most real limits are a \emph{curve} --- an exclusion as a function of mass, read off
a published figure. That is not something to hard-code into an expression, and
Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-operas} is the mechanism for it.

\subsection*{Combining terms}

Independent constraints add, which is what the automatic sum gives you. Two cases need a
deliberate choice instead:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correlated measurements.} A set of observables with a covariance matrix
        is one term, not $n$ terms: $\chi^2 = r^{\mathsf{T}} C^{-1} r$. Summing the
        diagonal pieces separately double-counts the shared systematic.
        \code{stat.chi2\_cov} (Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-operas}) computes it.
  \item \textbf{Alternative hypotheses.} When two channels are interpretations of the
        same signal rather than independent evidence, you want the better of the two, not
        their sum --- which is exactly the case for naming a term \code{LogL} yourself
        and writing \code{Max(...)} (next section).
\end{itemize}

\begin{jwarn}
Keep every term a \emph{log}-likelihood. Mixing a $\chi^2$ into the sum by accident
flips the sign of that constraint --- the scan will then walk uphill into the region the
measurement excludes, and nothing in the machinery will warn you, because a sum of
numbers is a valid sum of numbers. If a term comes from a $\chi^2$, write
\code{-0.5 * chi2}.
\end{jwarn}

\section{How \code{LogL} is formed}
\label{sec:logl-semantics}

Terms evaluate \emph{in order}, and each term's result is added to the observables before
the next term runs --- so later terms can reference earlier ones by name. Then:

\begin{itemize}
  \item If some term is literally named \code{LogL}, that term \textbf{is} the total ---
        no summing. Use this when you want full control:
\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  LogLikelihood:
    - name: LogL_a
      expression: LogGauss(obs_a, 5.1, 0.4)
    - name: LogL_b
      expression: LogGauss(obs_b, 0.7, 0.05)
    - name: LogL
      expression: Max(LogL_a, LogL_b)   # explicit total
\end{yamlwin}
  \item Otherwise, $\code{LogL} = \sum_i \text{term}_i$ --- the ordinary case where
        independent measurement terms add.
\end{itemize}

When the block is absent, the execution plan simply carries no likelihood
step and \code{LogL} is absent from the records --- legitimate for pure mapping scans
where you only want observables.

\begin{jnote}
Everything is \emph{log}-likelihood, natural log. The convenience kernels differ in their
normalisation: \code{LogGauss(x, mu, sigma)} is
$-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)^2$ without the constant term;
\code{Normal} includes the $1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$ factor; \code{Gauss} is the
unnormalised kernel $e^{-\frac{1}{2}((x-\mu)/\sigma)^2}$ (not a log!). See
Chapter~\ref{ch:expressions}.
\end{jnote}

\section{Physics functions you can call directly}
\label{sec:likelihood-operas}

The built-in catalog (Chapter~\ref{ch:expressions}) is deliberately generic --- logs,
trigonometry, \code{Gauss}, \code{Heaviside}. It contains no physics, because physics
does not belong in a language.

The physics lives in \Operas, and it reaches your likelihood the same way any other
function does: \textbf{a qualified name, callable directly inside an
\yamlkey{expression}}. There is no import, no declaration block, nothing to add to the
card. If the namespace is installed, the name works.

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  LogLikelihood:
    # a published exclusion curve, interpolated at this point's mass
    - name: LogL_dd
      expression: "-1e6 * Heaviside(sigma_SI
                   - dmddxn.LZSI2024(m_chi))"
    # a correlated set, as one chi-square term
    - name: LogL_ew
      expression: "-0.5 * stat.chi2_cov(residual, cov)"
\end{yamlwin}

This is the extension point for everything the manual cannot enumerate --- your
collaboration's private limit curves, a likelihood from a paper, an interface to an
external code. Table~\ref{tab:operas-namespaces} lists the namespaces that ship today:

command line rather than a printed list:

\begin{terminal}
\begin{jcmd}
Jarvis operas list               # every name, grouped by namespace
Jarvis operas info stat.chi2_cov # signature, arguments, examples
\end{jcmd}
\end{terminal}

\begin{jtip}
Registering your own exclusion curve is the highest-value thing most users do with
\Operas: a two-column data file becomes \code{yourns.YourLimit(mass)}, usable in every
card you ever write, with the interpolation handled for you.
Chapter~\ref{ch:jarvis-operas} covers registration, persistence, and how Workers
discover what you registered.
\end{jtip}

\section{Failure semantics}
\label{sec:likelihood-failure}

A likelihood expression that references an observable missing from the dictionary raises
a structured \emph{missing-variables} error naming the absent symbols, and the Sample
\textbf{fails} --- it is recorded as failed, keeps its logs, and the scan continues.
This is deliberate: a missing observable usually means an upstream calculator failed to
produce output for this point, and silently scoring the point would poison the scan.

If a term should be optional --- an observable only some points produce --- make the
producing module emit an explicit default instead of omitting the key
(Chapters~\ref{ch:calculators} and~\ref{ch:operas}: absent output entries are captured
as null values rather than dropped, so the expression sees \emph{something}).

\section{Who consumes \code{LogL}}
\label{sec:logl-consumers}

For the fixed-set methods, the likelihood is bookkeeping: every accepted point runs
regardless, and \code{LogL} lands in the database for your posterior analysis. Two
consumers act on it at runtime:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \sampler{AdaptiveBridson} evaluates its \yamlkey{target\_expression} over the
        returned observables --- including likelihood terms --- to steer refinement
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-levelset}).
  \item Your plots: \file{samples.csv} carries every term as a column, so per-term
        pulls are one \code{pandas} expression away.
\end{itemize}

\begin{jtip}
Name terms by what they constrain --- \code{LogL\_mh}, \code{LogL\_relic},
\code{LogL\_bsg} --- and let the automatic sum form the total. Per-term columns in the
output are the fastest way to answer ``which constraint kills this region?''.
\end{jtip}
