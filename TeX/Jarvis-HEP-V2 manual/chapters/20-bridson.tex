\chapter{Bridson}
\label{ch:bridson}

\newthought{Blue-noise coverage.} The \sampler{Bridson} method fills u-space with points
that are never closer than a chosen radius --- Poisson-disk sampling, generated with
Bridson's dart-throwing algorithm.\cite{Bridson:2007} The result covers space as evenly
as a grid but without its axis-aligned artifacts, and as irregularly as random draws but
without their clumps and voids. It is the recommended default for mapping a parameter
space you intend to interpolate, plot, or train a surrogate on.

\section{Configuration}
\label{sec:bridson-config}

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Method: Bridson
  Radius: 0.35            # REQUIRED: min u-space distance between points
  MaxAttempt: 30          # REQUIRED: candidates per active point (k)
  Seed: 42
  Variables:
    - name: x
      distribution: {type: Flat, parameters: {min: 0.0, max: 1.0, length: 1.0}}
    - name: y
      distribution: {type: Flat, parameters: {min: 0.0, max: 1.0, length: 1.0}}
\end{yamlwin}

\begin{keylist}
  \item[Radius] \req\ (\code{KeyError} if absent). The minimum distance between any two
        accepted points, \emph{in u-space}. This is the resolution dial: the expected
        point count scales like $(\mathrm{length}/\mathrm{Radius})^d$.
  \item[MaxAttempt] \req. Bridson's $k$ --- candidate darts thrown around each active
        point before it retires. $30$ is the standard literature value; it bounds the
        \emph{placement} algorithm only and has nothing to do with \yamlkey{selection}
        retries.
  \item[MaxWorker] \opt, default \yamlkey{EnvReqs.V2.workers}. Caps in-flight proposals
        during submission.
  \item[parameters.length] \req\ \emph{per variable} (\code{KeyError} if absent): the
        u-space box edge for that dimension. With \code{length: 1.0} everywhere the
        domain is the unit cube; a larger \yamlkey{length} stretches that dimension's
        share of the distance metric --- effectively finer sampling along it.
\end{keylist}

The point count is \emph{emergent}: the generator packs points until no more fit. After
generation the count is reported in the logs before submission starts
(\code{NSamples}), so abort early if it is not what you expected.

\begin{jwarn}
Both \yamlkey{Radius} and \yamlkey{length} are u-space quantities. Distances between
\emph{physical} points depend on each variable's distribution --- a \code{Log} variable
packs more physical resolution per unit of u near its lower bound. That is usually
exactly what you want; just do not read \yamlkey{Radius} as GeV.
\end{jwarn}

\section{How it behaves in the runtime}
\label{sec:bridson-runtime}

Generation happens once, at startup, in the control process; it is pure geometry and
costs seconds up to $\sim$$10^6$ points. Submission then streams the accepted set to
Redis in the usual batches, with progress logged. Each point's \textsc{uuid} is
deterministic in \code{(seed, index)}, and the generated disk set itself is part of the
sampler's checkpoint state --- resume re-submits only unfinished points of the
\emph{same} geometry rather than regenerating (Chapter~\ref{ch:checkpoint}).

\yamlkey{selection} applies after generation: rejected points consume their slot
(Section~\ref{sec:selection}), so a tight cut thins the blue-noise set --- the surviving
points are still well-spaced inside the allowed region.

\section{Choosing between Bridson and the alternatives}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{vs \sampler{Random}:} at equal budget, Bridson's worst empty void is
        dramatically smaller; interpolation and contour plots come out visibly cleaner.
  \item \textbf{vs \sampler{Grid}:} no axis alignment means no moiré artifacts in
        derived contours, and resolution degrades gracefully as $d$ grows instead of
        exploding as a product.
  \item \textbf{Dimension limit:} the generator is practical for $2 \le d \lesssim 4$;
        beyond that the packing count explodes (Table~\ref{tab:bridson-counts}) ---
        switch to \sampler{Random}.
  \item \textbf{Boundary hunting:} if what you actually want is a \emph{contour}, let
        \sampler{AdaptiveBridson} (next chapter) spend the same budget on the boundary
        instead of the bulk.
\end{itemize}

\begin{jtip}
Two-stage mapping works well: a coarse Bridson pass (\yamlkey{Radius} $\sim$0.1) to see
the landscape, then export the interesting region's points and refine --- either a finer
Bridson scan with tightened variable bounds, or a \sampler{CSV} replay with the heavy
workflow (Chapter~\ref{ch:csv-sampler}).
\end{jtip}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:bridson-summary}

\sampler{Bridson} is the default once you are past debugging: blue-noise coverage with
no axis artifacts and no combinatorial blow-up, at the cost of an emergent (not dialed)
point count and a $d \lesssim 4$ practical ceiling.
