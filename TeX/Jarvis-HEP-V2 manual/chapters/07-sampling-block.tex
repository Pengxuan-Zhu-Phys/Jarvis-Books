\chapter{The Sampling Block}
\label{ch:sampling-block}

\newthought{\yamlkey{Sampling} is where you describe the scan itself.} Everywhere else in
the card you are describing machinery --- which programs run, how files are parsed, how
many Workers there are. Here you describe the actual scientific question, and the block's
shape follows directly from what asking that question requires.

Before any scan can start, somebody has to answer four things:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item[Where do I look?] The parameter space --- which quantities vary, over what
        ranges, on what scale. That is \yamlkey{Variables}.
  \item[How do I move through it?] Uniform draws, an exhaustive grid, blue-noise
        packing, a contour chase, a nested-sampling run. That is \yamlkey{Method}.
  \item[How good is a point once I have it?] The score that says whether a set of
        parameter values agrees with your data. That is \yamlkey{LogLikelihood}.
  \item[Anything I should skip?] Regions that are unphysical, or points whose
        calculation fell over. That is \yamlkey{selection} and \yamlkey{on\_failure}.
\end{description}

Those four answers \emph{are} the top level of the block --- there is nothing else up
there, and nothing on the list you could sensibly leave to a default:

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Method: Bridson                     # how to move
  Variables:                          # where to look
    - name: mh
      distribution:
        type: Flat
        parameters: {min: 110.0, max: 140.0, length: 1.0}
    - name: tanb
      distribution:
        type: Log
        parameters: {min: 1.0, max: 60.0, length: 1.0}
  LogLikelihood:                      # how good is a point
    - {name: LogL_mh, expression: "LogGauss(mh, 125.25, 0.17)"}
  selection: "tanb > 2.0"             # what to skip
  Bounds:                             # settings only Bridson understands
    Radius: 0.35
    MaxAttempt: 30
    Seed: 42
\end{yamlwin}

\yamlkey{Bounds} is the one key that is not an answer to any of the four questions, and
that is exactly why it is a separate storey. A radius means nothing to \sampler{Grid}; a
live-point budget means nothing to \sampler{Random}. Those numbers belong to one method
rather than to the scan, so they get their own drawer (Section~\ref{sec:bounds}).

The payoff of that split is worth seeing before the details: it means \textbf{changing
how you scan does not disturb what you are scanning}. Swap \yamlkey{Method: Bridson} for
\yamlkey{Method: Dynesty}, replace the three lines of \yamlkey{Bounds} underneath it, and
your parameter space, your likelihood, and your cut are all still there, untouched and
still comparable. The scientific question survives a change of technique.

The top-level keys are all ones \Jtwo\ checks, so a misspelling among them stops the load
instead of being silently ignored. Whether a misspelling \emph{inside} \yamlkey{Bounds}
is caught depends on the method, and Section~\ref{sec:bounds} says exactly which ones
protect you.

This chapter walks the four questions in order, then \yamlkey{Bounds}. Each method's own
keys are detailed in Part~\ref{part:samplers}.

\section{\yamlkey{Variables}: where to look}
\label{sec:sampling-variables}

\yamlkey{Variables} is a list, one entry per quantity you want to vary. Each entry gives
the parameter a \yamlkey{name} --- the name your calculators, expressions, and output
columns will all use for it --- and a \yamlkey{distribution} saying what range it covers
and how the sampler should space points across it.

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Variables:
    - name: mh
      description: "Higgs mass"     # optional, for your own notes
      distribution:
        type: Flat                  # even spacing over [min, max]
        parameters: {min: 110.0, max: 140.0, length: 1.0}
    - name: tanb
      distribution:
        type: Log                   # even spacing in log10
        parameters: {min: 1.0, max: 60.0, length: 1.0}
\end{yamlwin}

The choice of \yamlkey{type} is a scientific statement, not a formatting one: declaring
\yamlkey{Log} for a quantity you think of logarithmically means the sampler spends its
budget evenly across decades instead of piling almost every point into the top decade.
Getting this right costs one word and is usually worth more than any amount of sampler
tuning.

Two \yamlkey{parameters} entries are required only by particular methods ---
\yamlkey{num} by \sampler{Grid}, \yamlkey{length} by \sampler{Bridson} --- and they are
described with those methods. The full distribution catalog, the required list of
\yamlkey{parameters} for each \yamlkey{type}, and the rules on names live in
Chapter~\ref{ch:variables}.

An empty or missing \yamlkey{Variables} list is an error for every method that generates
its own points. \sampler{CSV} is the exception: it reads physical points from a file, so
its columns \emph{are} the parameters and this key is not consulted at all.

\section{\yamlkey{Method}: how to move through it}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Method: Bridson    # Bridson | Random | Grid | CSV
                     # | AdaptiveBridson | Dynesty | MultiNest
\end{yamlwin}

Seven methods have a dedicated chapter and are the focus of this book (list below). Ten
more --- the \textsc{mcmc} family --- are implemented and selectable, just without a
closed \textsc{yaml} contract or chapter of their own yet; Chapter~\ref{ch:choosing-sampler}
covers what is confirmed about them. Alternatively,
\yamlkey{Sampling.mode: check\_modules} replaces \yamlkey{Method} resolution and runs
the workflow smoke path of Chapter~\ref{ch:check-modules}.

A name \Jtwo\ does not implement fails validation before anything starts, and the message
lists every name it does implement --- so you never need to guess the spelling:

\begin{terminal}
\begin{jout}
[error] JV2-MTH-003  Sampling.Method
    'Bridsonn' is not implemented in Jarvis-HEP V2. Available:
    AM, AMMCMC, AdaptiveBridson, Bridson, CSV, DEMCMC, DRAM,
    Dynesty, Ensemble, EnsembleMCMC, Grid, MCMC, MultiNest, PT,
    PTEnsemble, PTMCMC, Random
\end{jout}
\end{terminal}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item[\sampler{Random}] fixed set --- uniform i.i.d.\ draws in u-space
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:random}).
  \item[\sampler{Grid}] fixed set --- exhaustive Cartesian product
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:grid}).
  \item[\sampler{CSV}] fixed set --- replay physical points from a file
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:csv-sampler}).
  \item[\sampler{Bridson}] fixed set --- blue-noise (Poisson-disk) space filling
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:bridson}).
  \item[\sampler{AdaptiveBridson}] feedback --- iterative refinement around a level set
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-levelset}).
  \item[\sampler{MultiNest}] nested (static) --- evidence + posterior, fixed
        live-point budget (Chapter~\ref{ch:multinest}).
  \item[\sampler{Dynesty}] nested (dynamic) --- evidence + posterior, adaptive
        live-point batches (Chapter~\ref{ch:dynesty}).
\end{description}

``Fixed set'' methods generate their whole point set up front;
\sampler{AdaptiveBridson} reacts to results (Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-levelset}); the
nested methods react to results toward an evidence estimate
(Chapter~\ref{ch:choosing-sampler}). New methods land in this list as they ship ---
check here first for the current, authoritative set.

\section{\yamlkey{LogLikelihood}: how good is a point}
\label{sec:sampling-loglikelihood}

Your workflow turns a set of parameter values into observables --- a mass, a cross
section, a relic density. \yamlkey{LogLikelihood} is where you say what those observables
are \emph{worth}: a list of named expressions, each computing one log-likelihood
contribution from the observables the workflow produced.

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  LogLikelihood:                      # alias: loglikelihood
    - name: LogL_mh
      expression: "LogGauss(mh, 125.25, 0.17)"
    - name: LogL_relic
      expression: "LogGauss(Omega_h2, 0.120, 0.001)"
\end{yamlwin}

\begin{keylist}
  \item[name] \opt, default \code{LogL}. The term's name. It becomes an observable in its
        own right --- it is written to the output records as a column, and later terms in
        the list can refer to it by name.
  \item[expression] \req. An expression in the shared language of
        Chapter~\ref{ch:expressions}, evaluated over the observables.
\end{keylist}

Terms are summed into a total called \code{LogL}, which is the number every sampler
actually reads. Two consequences you should know while writing the block, both covered
properly in Chapter~\ref{ch:loglikelihood}: terms evaluate \emph{in order}, so a later
term may use an earlier one's result; and if you name a term \code{LogL} yourself, that
term \emph{becomes} the total instead of being added to it --- which is how you write a
likelihood that is not a plain sum.

\subsection*{Why it lives here and not at the top of the card}

The likelihood is not a separate stage of the run; it is the sampler's feedback signal.
\sampler{Dynesty} decides which region to refine from \code{LogL}, an \textsc{mcmc} chain
accepts or rejects a step from \code{LogL}, and \sampler{AdaptiveBridson} chases a
contour defined over the same observables. Putting the score in the same block as the
method that consumes it keeps that relationship visible --- and it means one block holds
everything you would need to compare on if you re-ran the same physics with a different
sampler.

\begin{jnote}
The block is optional. Leave it out and the run simply carries no likelihood step:
\code{LogL} is absent from the records and you get a pure map of observables over the
parameter space. That is a legitimate and common way to use \Jtwo\ --- but note that the
methods which \emph{steer} on results (the nested and \textsc{mcmc} families) have
nothing to steer on without it, so pair a likelihood-free card with a fixed-set method.
\end{jnote}

\begin{jwarn}
A likelihood block at the \emph{top level} of the card --- \yamlkey{Likelihood:} beside
\yamlkey{Sampling} and \yamlkey{Operas} --- is not a \Jtwo\ interface and stops the load.
\yamlkey{Sampling.LogLikelihood} is the one place likelihood terms live.
\end{jwarn}

\section{\yamlkey{selection} and \yamlkey{on\_failure}: what to skip}
\label{sec:selection}

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Method: Random
  selection: "x + y < 1.5"
  Bounds:
    "Point number": 500
\end{yamlwin}

The expression uses the shared language of Chapter~\ref{ch:expressions}, evaluated per
candidate over the physical parameter values. Its interaction with the sampling budget is
the same for all fixed-set methods, and is worth stating plainly:

\begin{jwarn}
Fixed-set methods generate their point set \emph{once} ---
\yamlkey{Bounds."Point number"} draws, the full grid, or the Bridson disk set. A candidate rejected by
\yamlkey{selection} \textbf{consumes its slot}: there is no redraw, no retry, no top-up.
A restrictive cut therefore yields \emph{fewer accepted samples than the nominal budget}.
If you need $N$ accepted points inside a cut of acceptance $\varepsilon$, budget
$N/\varepsilon$.
\end{jwarn}

For \sampler{AdaptiveBridson}, the cut filters proposals before submission in the same
way; dropped points are simply never scheduled, and never counted as failures.

Each distinct selection expression is compiled once per process and cached; per-candidate
evaluation is a single numerical call, so even million-point sets filter quickly.

\subsection*{\yamlkey{on\_failure}: when a point never produced a score}

\yamlkey{selection} skips points you decided not to want. \yamlkey{on\_failure} covers the
other case: a point you \emph{did} want, whose likelihood never evaluated --- a calculator
crashed, timed out, or returned something the expression could not use.

\begin{keylist}
  \item[reject] the default. Treat the point as infinitely bad and carry on. The scan
        completes; the failure is recorded and visible in the logs.
  \item[halt] stop the whole scan at the first such point.
\end{keylist}

Use \code{halt} while you are still commissioning a workflow, when a failure almost
certainly means your setup is wrong and a thousand more failures will not tell you
anything new. Switch to \code{reject} for production, where a handful of pathological
parameter points is expected and should not cost you the run.

Only the methods that \emph{steer} on results consult it --- an \textsc{mcmc} chain has to
know what to do when the score it needs did not arrive. Fixed-set methods never read it,
because their point set was decided before any result came back.

\section{The top level in full}
\label{sec:common-keys}

The four questions account for the keys you will write on almost every card. For
reference, here is the complete top level --- if a sampler setting is not on this list, it
belongs in \yamlkey{Bounds}.

\begin{keylist}
  \item[Method] \req\ (unless \yamlkey{mode: check\_modules}); alias \yamlkey{method}.
        Section~\ref{sec:method}.
  \item[Variables] \req\ for \sampler{Bridson}, \sampler{Random}, \sampler{Grid}, and
        \sampler{AdaptiveBridson} (an empty list is an error); ignored by \sampler{CSV},
        which names its columns in \yamlkey{Bounds}.
        Section~\ref{sec:sampling-variables}; distribution catalog in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:variables}.
  \item[Mapper] \opt. Derive further parameters from the sampling variables with
        expressions, when the coordinates you want to sample are not the ones the
        workflow needs. Section~\ref{sec:mapper}.
  \item[LogLikelihood] \opt, alias \yamlkey{loglikelihood}. The list of likelihood terms
        that score each point. Section~\ref{sec:sampling-loglikelihood}; the likelihood
        itself in Chapter~\ref{ch:loglikelihood}.
  \item[selection] \opt. A boolean expression over the \emph{physical} parameters; a
        point failing the cut is dropped before submission.
        Section~\ref{sec:selection}.
  \item[on\_failure] \opt, \code{reject} (default) or \code{halt}. What to do with a
        Sample whose likelihood did not evaluate; \yamlkey{Bounds.on\_failure} overrides
        it. Section~\ref{sec:selection}.
  \item[Bounds] The method's own settings (Section~\ref{sec:bounds}). Required by
        \sampler{Bridson}, \sampler{Random}, \sampler{CSV}, and
        \sampler{AdaptiveBridson}; optional for everything else.
  \item[Nuisance] \opt. Profile a nuisance parameter inside each Sample
        (Section~\ref{sec:nuisance}).
\end{keylist}

\begin{jnote}
\yamlkey{Seed} is \emph{not} on this list. It used to sit at the top level, and it now
lives in \yamlkey{Bounds} with the rest of a method's settings --- see
Section~\ref{sec:bounds}, which also shows the error you get if you write it in the old
place.
\end{jnote}

\section{\yamlkey{Bounds}: where a method's own settings live}
\label{sec:bounds}

Every sampler needs numbers that mean nothing to the others. A radius is meaningless to
\sampler{Grid}; a live-point count is meaningless to \sampler{Random}; \code{nlive: 400}
next to \code{Radius: 0.35} would just be two methods' settings in one drawer, and only
one of them would ever be read. \yamlkey{Bounds} is that drawer, one per card, holding
whichever method you actually named:

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Method: Random
  Bounds:
    "Point number": 500
    Seed: 11
\end{yamlwin}

Keeping them here is what makes a method swap a two-line edit: \yamlkey{Method} and the
block underneath it are adjacent, and everything above them --- the parameter space, the
likelihood, the cut --- is untouched.

\subsection*{The settings that used to live at the top level}

\yamlkey{Radius}, \yamlkey{MaxAttempt}, \yamlkey{MaxWorker}, \yamlkey{"Point number"},
\yamlkey{Seed}, the \yamlkey{CSV} sub-block, and the \yamlkey{AdaptiveBridson} sub-block
were once written directly under \yamlkey{Sampling}. They are read from \yamlkey{Bounds}
only. A card written the old way does not run half-configured: validation names the key,
names where it goes now, and shows you the replacement:

\begin{terminal}
\begin{jout}
[error] JV2-MTH-001  Sampling.Radius
    Sampling.Radius is not a V2 setting; place it under
    Sampling.Bounds.Radius
    hint: Method-specific sampler settings belong exclusively
          under Sampling.Bounds.
    example:
      Bounds:
        Radius: 0.1
        MaxAttempt: 30
\end{jout}
\end{terminal}

The two sub-blocks flatten rather than move. There is no
\yamlkey{Bounds.AdaptiveBridson} and no \yamlkey{Bounds.CSV}: an
\sampler{AdaptiveBridson} card writes \yamlkey{target\_expression} directly in
\yamlkey{Bounds}, and a \sampler{CSV} card writes \yamlkey{path} directly in
\yamlkey{Bounds}.

\subsection*{\yamlkey{Bounds.Seed}}

\yamlkey{Seed} (alias \yamlkey{seed}; the nested engines also accept \yamlkey{rseed})
is the one \yamlkey{Bounds} key nearly every method understands, which is why it appears
in this chapter rather than in seven others. It is an integer, it defaults to \code{0},
and \code{0} is a real seed like any other --- the stream is seeded with it, so two runs
of the same card produce the same points whether you wrote \code{Seed: 0} or nothing at
all. Sample \textsc{uuid}s are derived from the seed and the point index, which is what
makes resume and replay stable (Chapter~\ref{ch:reproducibility}).

\subsection*{Which methods check what you write inside}

\begin{keylist}
  \item[Checked, strictly] \sampler{Random}, \sampler{Grid}, \sampler{Bridson},
        \sampler{CSV}, \sampler{AdaptiveBridson}. Each has a closed list of accepted
        keys, so \code{Radius: 0.1} under \sampler{Random}, or \code{Radus: 0.1} under
        \sampler{Bridson}, is an error you are told about before the scan starts.
  \item[Passed through] \sampler{Dynesty}, \sampler{MultiNest}, and the \textsc{mcmc}
        family. Their \yamlkey{Bounds} is largely a set of arguments for the engine
        underneath, whose vocabulary is not \Jtwo's to police; the keys \Jtwo\ does
        understand are checked, the rest travel as written. A typo there is yours to
        catch. Chapters~\ref{ch:multinest} and~\ref{ch:dynesty} list what is understood.
\end{keylist}

If you are new to the system, note that this is the good direction to be surprised in:
the four methods you are most likely to start with are the four that will not let a
mistyped key through.


\section{\yamlkey{Nuisance}: profiling a parameter inside each Sample}
\label{sec:nuisance}

Some parameters are not ones you want to scan over. A luminosity scale, a form-factor
normalisation, a theory error --- you want the \emph{best} value of it for each physics
point, not a dimension of u-space spent on it. \yamlkey{Nuisance} does that: for every
Sample, it searches one extra parameter over a range and keeps the score at its optimum.

\begin{yamlwin}
Sampling:
  Nuisance:
    Method: Profile1D
    Variables:
      - name: z
        distribution:
          type: Flat
          parameters: {min: -3.0, max: 3.0}
    LogLikelihood:
      - {name: nuis_pull, expression: "-0.5*z**2"}
    PassCondition:
      - {name: in_band, expression: "mh > 122 and mh < 128"}
    TargetMode: min
    MaxAttempt: 50
    re_run_physics: true
\end{yamlwin}

\begin{keylist}
  \item[Method] \opt, default \code{Profile1D}. One method ships, and it is a
        golden-section search over a single parameter.
  \item[Variables] \req. Only the \emph{first} entry is used, and only its
        \yamlkey{name} and \yamlkey{parameters.min}/\yamlkey{max} --- they set the search
        interval. The distribution \yamlkey{type} is not consulted.
  \item[LogLikelihood] \opt, alias \yamlkey{loglikelihood}. Named expressions summed into
        the score being optimised. This is where a Gaussian pull on the nuisance parameter
        goes.
  \item[PassCondition] \opt, alias \yamlkey{pass\_condition}. Named boolean expressions;
        a probe that fails any of them is not eligible to be the optimum.
  \item[TargetMode] \opt, alias \yamlkey{target\_mode}, default \code{min}. Whether the
        search minimises or maximises (\code{max}) the score.
  \item[MaxAttempt] \opt, alias \yamlkey{max\_attempt}, default \code{50}. The probe
        budget per Sample.
  \item[re\_run\_physics] \opt, alias \yamlkey{rerun\_physics}, default \code{true}.
        Whether each probe re-runs the calculators and operas with the new nuisance value.
        Leave it true when the nuisance parameter feeds your physics programs; set it
        false when the nuisance appears only in expressions, which makes each probe
        almost free.
\end{keylist}

\begin{jwarn}
The cost multiplies. With \yamlkey{re\_run\_physics: true} --- the default --- a Sample
runs its whole workflow once \emph{per probe}, so a scan of $N$ points with
\yamlkey{MaxAttempt: 50} can dispatch up to $50N$ calculator runs. Set
\yamlkey{re\_run\_physics: false} whenever the nuisance parameter never reaches a
calculator, and reduce \yamlkey{MaxAttempt} before reaching for more Workers.
\end{jwarn}

\section{How samples reach the Workers}
\label{sec:submission}

Whatever the method, submission is uniform: the sampler walks its accepted points, mints
each a deterministic \textsc{uuid}, and pushes light task dictionaries to Redis in groups
of \yamlkey{EnvReqs.V2.batch\_size}. Fixed-set methods submit everything and wait;
\sampler{AdaptiveBridson} submits a generation, waits at a barrier for results, and
decides the next one. Workers do not know or care which method proposed the task ---
everything method-specific stays in the control process.

\begin{jtip}
Start every new card as \sampler{Random} with a small \yamlkey{Bounds."Point number"} and
your real workflow. It exercises the same execution path as any other method, so you can debug
calculators and likelihood cheaply, then switch \yamlkey{Method} when the physics is
right. Chapter~\ref{ch:choosing-sampler} gives the decision guide.
\end{jtip}
