\chapter{The Task Card}
\label{ch:task-yaml}

\newthought{Six keys. That is the entire top-level vocabulary of a task card}, and
everything in Part~\ref{part:task-card} is a detail of one of them. This chapter is the
hub: it shows the shape of a card, explains why the format is strict in some places and
deliberately not in others, lists every key the card accepts, and --- most usefully ---
teaches you to read the report \Jtwo\ prints when a card is wrong, because that report
names the exact key and usually shows you the corrected \textsc{yaml}. Read this chapter
once and you will know which of the following chapters to open, and when.

\section{The shape of a card}
\label{sec:card-shape}

A task card is one \textsc{yaml} document whose top level is a mapping. Exactly six keys
are allowed there, all of them optional as far as the format is concerned:

\begin{terminal}[the whole top-level vocabulary]
\begin{jout}
Scan:         name the scan and say where its outputs go
Sampling:     which method, which variables, which likelihood
EnvReqs:      environment checks and the V2 runtime block
LibDeps:      external tool paths and registered executables
Calculators:  external programs to run per point
Operas:       in-process operators to call per point
\end{jout}
\end{terminal}

A misspelling is not a silent no-op. \code{Calculater} is an error, reported before the
scan can start, with the six legal names printed next to it. The leanest card that does
useful work needs only two of the six --- a \yamlkey{Sampling} block and something to
evaluate, exactly as in the quick start:

\begin{yamlwin}
Scan:
  name: quickstart
Sampling:
  Method: Random
  "Point number": 20
  Variables:
    - name: x
      distribution: {type: Flat, parameters: {min: 0.0, max: 1.0}}
  LogLikelihood:
    - {name: LogL_Z, expression: z}
Operas:
  Modules:
    - name: TrivialEggbox
      operator: jarvishep2.testing.eggbox.eggbox2d_numpy
      input:  [{name: x, expression: x}, {name: y, expression: y}]
      output: [{name: z, entry: z}]
\end{yamlwin}

Everything else in Part~\ref{part:task-card} adds to that skeleton without changing its
shape. If a card ever looks intimidating, find the six top-level keys first: the rest is
always a subtree of one of them.

\section{Strict where it knows, honest where it does not}
\label{sec:zones}

The natural worry about a format this large is that you will be punished for a typo
somewhere deep in it. The design's answer is to be strict \emph{on purpose and only where
that strictness is earned}. Every object in the card carries an ownership marker, and
there are exactly three:

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.50\linewidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Zone} & \textbf{Who owns it} & \textbf{What happens to a key you invent} \\
    \midrule
    closed     & \Jtwo & Error, naming the key and listing the legal ones. \\
    delegated  & another tool & Passed through. \Jtwo\ checks only the envelope it
                 declared, never the contents. \\
    open       & nobody, yet & Passed through with one warning that says so, and why. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption[The three ownership zones.]{The three ownership zones. Which one an object is
  in decides whether an unfamiliar key is an error, a pass-through, or a warning.}
  \label{tab:zones}
\end{table}

The distinction is worth an example, because it is the difference between two very
similar-looking mistakes.

\paragraph{A closed zone catches you.} \yamlkey{Sampling} is closed: \Jtwo\ knows every
key that belongs there. Write \yamlkey{Sampling.Radus} and validation stops you
immediately with the correct spelling in hand. You never get to find out at minute forty
of a scan that your \yamlkey{Radius} was never read.

\paragraph{A delegated zone gets out of your way.} \yamlkey{Sampling.Bounds} is
delegated, because the keys inside it belong to \code{dynesty}, not to \Jtwo. Pretending
to know that vocabulary would mean a new \code{dynesty} release could make a perfectly
valid card fail against a stale allow-list. So \Jtwo\ validates that \yamlkey{Bounds} is a
mapping and hands the contents on. The cost is honest and stated: a typo \emph{inside}
\yamlkey{Bounds} is between you and \code{dynesty}
(Chapter~\ref{ch:multinest} shows what that looks like).

\begin{jnote}
This is the whole psychology of the format. \Jtwo\ is strict about the parts it wrote and
silent about the parts it did not, and the card tells you which is which. You are never
guessing whether a key was understood --- either it was checked, or it was explicitly
delegated to a tool that will check it.
\end{jnote}

\section{How a card is loaded}
\label{sec:card-loading}

\cli{Jarvis2 run}, \cli{Jarvis2 check} and \cli{Jarvis2 validate} all put the card through
the same five layers, in this order. Knowing the order tells you which layer produced a
complaint, and therefore what kind of fix it wants:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Parse.} \code{yaml.safe\_load}; the top level must be a mapping.
        Failures here are syntax, not semantics --- a stray tab, an unclosed quote.
  \item \textbf{Character gate.} Keys and string values in your document must be
        \textsc{ascii}. This catches the classic disaster of a curly quote or a full-width
        colon pasted in from a chat window, which \textsc{yaml} accepts and no program
        can use.
  \item \textbf{Normalisation.} Historical spellings are folded to their modern
        equivalents, defaults are filled in, and the loader stamps in the resolved paths
        (project root, output root, scan name --- Section~\ref{sec:projects}).
  \item \textbf{Schema.} The structure, the types, and the ownership zones of
        Section~\ref{sec:zones}. This is where an unknown key or a string-where-a-number
        -belongs is caught.
  \item \textbf{Contracts.} Everything a schema cannot express: that \code{min} is below
        \code{max}, that a required key exists \emph{for the method you chose}, that a
        path resolves, that an operator can be imported.
\end{enumerate}

Layers 4 and 5 report into one combined report, so you never fix a schema error, re-run,
and only then discover a contract error underneath it.

\section{Reading a validation report}
\label{sec:validation}

\cli{Jarvis2 validate <card>.yaml} runs layers 1--5 and starts nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:cli-validate}). A card that passes says so in one line. A card that
fails prints the same report every time, in three parts:

\begin{terminal}
\begin{jcmd}
Jarvis2 validate bin/Example_Bridson.yaml
\end{jcmd}
\termsep
\begin{jout}
Config validation failed (2 error(s), 0 warning(s)):

Error summary (reference only):
+---+-------------+-----------------+----------------------------------------+
| # | Code        | YAML path       | Problem                                |
+---+-------------+-----------------+----------------------------------------+
| 1 | JV2-SCH-001 | $               | Additional properties are not allowed  |
| 2 | JV2-MTH-003 | Sampling.Method | 'Bridsonn' is not implemented          |
+---+-------------+-----------------+----------------------------------------+

Detailed diagnostics:
  [error] JV2-MTH-003  Sampling.Method
          'Bridsonn' is not implemented in Jarvis-HEP V2. Available: AM,
          AMMCMC, AdaptiveBridson, Bridson, CSV, DEMCMC, DRAM, Dynesty, ...
          suggestion: Choose an implemented Method, then provide its
          required configuration fields.
          example:
            Sampling:
              Method: Random
              Point number: 100
              Variables:
                - name: x
                  distribution:
                    type: Flat
                    parameters: {min: 0.0, max: 1.0}
\end{jout}
\end{terminal}

The three parts do different jobs, and the third is the one to read:

\begin{keylist}
  \item[the count line] How many errors and how many warnings. Warnings do not stop the
        run unless you passed \cli{--strict}.
  \item[the summary table] One line per problem, for scanning. It is labelled
        \emph{reference only} because a truncated message is not enough to fix anything.
  \item[the detailed diagnostics] The real content: the \textbf{code}, the exact
        \textsc{yaml} \textbf{path}, the message, and --- where the validator knows the
        answer --- a \code{suggestion} in words and an \code{example} of correct
        \textsc{yaml} you can paste.
\end{keylist}

That last point is the part worth internalising. For a large class of mistakes you do not
have to come back to this book at all: the validator names the key, tells you what to do,
and prints the fix.

\subsection{The code tells you which chapter to open}
\label{sec:code-families}

Every finding carries a stable code of the form \code{JV2-<family>-<number>}. The codes
themselves are contract-stable, so they are safe to grep for in \textsc{ci} logs --- but
their more useful property is that \textbf{the family names the part of the card that is
unhappy}, and therefore the chapter that explains it:

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.50\linewidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Family} & \textbf{Where} & \textbf{Go to} \\
    \midrule
    \code{JV2-YAML-*} & the file itself & the parse failed; fix the \textsc{yaml} syntax \\
    \code{JV2-ENC-*}  & the file itself & a non-\textsc{ascii} key or string value \\
    \code{JV2-SCH-*}  & any block & unknown key or wrong type (\S\ref{sec:zones}) \\
    \code{JV2-LOAD-*} & the file itself & the card could not be loaded at all \\
    \code{JV2-ENV-*}  & \yamlkey{EnvReqs} & Chapter~\ref{ch:envreqs} \\
    \code{JV2-MTH-*}  & \yamlkey{Sampling.Method} and its per-method keys &
      Chapter~\ref{ch:sampling-block}, then that method's chapter in
      Part~\ref{part:samplers} \\
    \code{JV2-VAR-*}  & \yamlkey{Sampling.Variables} & Chapter~\ref{ch:variables} \\
    \code{JV2-BND-*}  & \yamlkey{Sampling.Bounds} & Chapter~\ref{ch:multinest},
      Chapter~\ref{ch:dynesty} \\
    \code{JV2-MOD-*}  & \yamlkey{Calculators.Modules} & Chapter~\ref{ch:calculators} \\
    \code{JV2-ARC-*}  & archiver settings & Chapter~\ref{ch:envreqs} \\
    \code{JV2-OPR-*}  & \yamlkey{Operas} & Chapter~\ref{ch:operas} \\
    \code{JV2-DEAD-*} & anywhere & warning: the key is accepted and then ignored \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption[Code families and the chapter that explains them.]{Code families and the
  chapter that explains them. The family in a failing code is the fastest index into
  Part~\ref{part:task-card} this book has.}
  \label{tab:code-families}
\end{table}

\begin{jtip}
Two codes are worth recognising on sight. \code{JV2-SCH-001} is \emph{unknown key} --- the
suggestion lists every legal key at that level, so it is nearly always a typo you can fix
without leaving the terminal. \code{JV2-DEAD-*} is the opposite shape: the key is spelled
correctly, is accepted, and does nothing. Run with \cli{--strict} and it becomes an error,
which is how you find settings you believe are taking effect and are not.
\end{jtip}

\section{Where to go from here}
\label{sec:part2-map}

Part~\ref{part:task-card} takes the six keys one at a time, in the order you meet them
when building a card:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:envreqs}} --- \yamlkey{EnvReqs}: shared environment
        defaults, and the \yamlkey{V2} block that sets Workers, batching, archiving and
        the sample-directory policy.
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:sampling-block}} --- \yamlkey{Sampling}: choosing a
        \yamlkey{Method}, and the keys every method shares.
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:variables}} --- \yamlkey{Sampling.Variables} and the
        distribution catalog: the declaration that defines your u-space
        (Section~\ref{sec:uspace}).
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:loglikelihood}} --- \yamlkey{Sampling.LogLikelihood}:
        how points are scored, and what \code{LogL} means.
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:expressions}} --- the expression language shared by every
        \yamlkey{expression:} field in the card.
\end{itemize}

The three workflow blocks --- \yamlkey{LibDeps}, \yamlkey{Calculators},
\yamlkey{Operas} --- are large enough to have their own part
(Part~\ref{part:backends}), and the per-method \yamlkey{Sampling} keys get
Part~\ref{part:samplers}. When you are not sure which of those a key belongs to, the
index in Section~\ref{app:yaml-index} names the chapter for every key in the card.
